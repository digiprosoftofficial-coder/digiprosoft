Inventory is where most pharmacies quietly lose money. Not through one big mistake, but through a steady stream of small ones: a box of antibiotics that expired on the back shelf, a popular painkiller that ran out on a Friday evening, a supplier bill paid twice because nobody could find the first receipt. Pharmacy management software exists to remove exactly these problems. This article explains how, in practical terms.

Why pharmacy inventory is harder than ordinary retail

A grocery shop tracks items. A pharmacy tracks items, batches, expiry dates, units and regulatory information — all at once. Three things make it unusually difficult:

  • Batches and expiry. The same medicine sits on the shelf in several batches with different expiry dates and sometimes different purchase prices. Selling the newest batch first while an older one expires is a common, invisible loss.
  • Units. Medicines are bought in boxes, stocked in strips and sold in strips or single tablets. If the system does not convert units correctly, stock counts drift within days.
  • Volume. A mid-sized pharmacy in Dhaka can carry several thousand SKUs. Manual counting is not realistic more than once or twice a year.

Generic shop software rarely models batches or unit conversion properly, which is why many pharmacies fall back to notebooks and memory.

1. Batch-level stock with first-expiry-first-out

Good pharmacy software stores every purchase as a batch with its own expiry date and cost. At the counter, when a medicine is scanned or searched, the system suggests the batch that expires first — known as FEFO (first expiry, first out). Staff do not have to check dates by hand; the default is already the right one.

The same data makes expiry reports possible: a list of everything expiring in the next 30, 60 or 90 days. That list is the difference between returning stock to a supplier in time and writing it off.

2. Reorder levels that reflect real sales

Stock-outs are the other side of the problem. Software records every sale, so it knows how fast each item moves. Set a minimum level per item — or let the system suggest one from recent sales — and you get a daily low-stock list to base purchase orders on. Fast movers stay available; slow movers stop tying up cash.

3. Accurate unit conversion

A system built for pharmacies lets you define units per item: 1 box = 10 strips = 100 tablets, with a price for each. Sell 3 tablets and stock decreases by 0.03 boxes automatically. This sounds trivial but it is the single most common reason stock counts go wrong in shops that use generic software.

4. Purchases, returns and supplier dues in one ledger

When purchase invoices are entered into the system, stock, cost price and supplier balance update together. Returns to suppliers reduce both stock and dues. At any moment you can see what you owe each supplier and what they owe you in returns — without a separate notebook.

5. Reports that show where money is tied up

Once stock and sales are accurate, useful reports become possible:

  • Stock valuation — how much capital is sitting on shelves, at cost.
  • Slow-moving items — products with no sales in 60 or 90 days.
  • Item-wise profit — margin per medicine, not just total sales.
  • Near-expiry value — the taka value of stock expiring soon, so it can be prioritised.

6. Controls that reduce leakage

Role-based access means counter staff can bill but not delete sales or change prices. An audit log records who edited what. Stock adjustments require a reason. None of this accuses anyone; it simply makes the numbers trustworthy.

What to look for when choosing pharmacy software

If you are evaluating systems, ask these questions:

  1. Does it track stock by batch and expiry, or only by item?
  2. Can I define multiple units per item with separate prices?
  3. Does the billing screen work quickly with a keyboard and barcode scanner?
  4. Does it keep working during internet outages?
  5. Are supplier dues and customer credit sales included?
  6. Can I get item-wise profit and near-expiry reports?
  7. Is training and local support included?

Summary

Pharmacy inventory problems are structural: batches, expiry, units and volume overwhelm manual methods. Software designed for pharmacies solves them by recording stock at batch level, suggesting the right batch at the counter, tracking units precisely and turning sales history into reorder decisions. The result is less expired stock, fewer stock-outs and a clear view of real profit.
